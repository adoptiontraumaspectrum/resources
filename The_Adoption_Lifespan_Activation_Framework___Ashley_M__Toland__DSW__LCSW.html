Where it began

It started as an observation.

The Spectrum came first. Before there was a framework, there was a pattern that is easy to miss: adoptees are not carrying a single wound from a single moment.

The pattern's core claim is that adoption trauma is an imprint, not an incident. It reactivates rather than simply lingers, and it does so non-linearly. Someone can integrate one activation point while another reopens years later.

Trauma is not only the original event. It is an imprint that reopens at later relational and identity-shaping moments.

How the framework grew

From a pattern to a framework

As the Spectrum unfolded, more questions began to surface. Why do these points reactivate? What established scholarship accounts for it? And how do these reactivations shape a person's development over time?

The framework grew up around the Spectrum to answer those three questions. It keeps the Spectrum at the center as the working mechanism, is grounded in strong theoretical foundations, and reveals a developmental pathway with psychosocial impacts and outcomes.

The Spectrum is the engine; the framework is the vehicle engineered around it.

The Logic

grounds
Foundations → Spectrum

The Spectrum is not a standalone idea. It is the place where several respected theories converge: trauma and loss on one side, attachment and identity on the other.

That convergence is what grounds means: the model rests on established scholarship, not on a single new claim.

moderates
Spectrum → Domains

In research terms, a moderator changes the strength or direction of the relationship between two things. A person does not travel in a straight line from that early rupture to a fixed outcome.

The activation points sit in the middle and shape how the path unfolds. That is why the outcome is a continuum, not a verdict.

The Spectrum reveals predictable activation points upon which adoption trauma may arise across the lifespan. The Framework examines the how of the lifelong impact of adoption trauma.
